The laughing gas crisis hiding in plain sight among Britain's South Asian youth

By Mustafa Hameed  ·  Project Coordinator, The Salik Project UK  ·  November 2026

In Rochdale, I have encountered young people who see laughing gas as a relatively harmless escape, and others who believe it is less religiously objectionable than cannabis. But across Manchester, Birmingham and London, the medical evidence tells a very different story. Why are we not talking about this?

Not long ago, I spoke to a young South Asian man in Rochdale who had started using nitrous oxide in his teens. He had been hospitalised several times and was now permanently disabled. Yet what stayed with me was not simply the extent of his injuries, but the remarkably ordinary explanation he gave for taking the drug in the first place.

“It makes me feel happy when am depressed, i live in Rochdale. There's nothing to do. My home life is shit, so i take it to feel better.”

I have returned to those words repeatedly. Here was a young man whose life had been permanently altered by a substance he had initially used to escape his unhappiness. Despite everything he had been through, he had not accessed recovery support.

Over the past year, through our work at The Salik Project UK, I have become increasingly interested in how nitrous oxide has found a place within parts of British South Asian youth culture. There is something happening here that I suspect many parents, community leaders and even some of the professionals commissioned to address substance use do not fully understand.

During our focus groups in Rochdale, young participants have described seeing South Asian girls sitting in cars inhaling nitrous oxide from balloons. Some recounted these encounters with a degree of astonishment, as though the sight of young Asian women taking drugs was somehow more extraordinary than young men doing the same.

I found their reactions almost as interesting as the behaviour they were describing.

One evening, after delivering a focus group, we returned to our house in Rochdale and noticed a South Asian teenage girl, dressed in her nightgown, discreetly inhaling from a balloon. She appeared to be keeping an eye out for members of her family. When she noticed us, she hurriedly discarded the balloon and ran inside.

It happened in seconds, but the encounter has stayed with me.

I knew nothing about her circumstances, and it would be wrong to draw conclusions about her drug use from a single incident. But the secrecy was striking. Here was a teenager apparently negotiating two competing worlds: the culture of her peers, in which inhaling nitrous oxide appeared sufficiently familiar to happen close to home, and the expectations of her family, from whom she seemed determined to conceal it.

The incident also made me think about the particular pressures placed upon young South Asian women. In some families, girls are subject to scrutiny and restrictions that their brothers may never experience. The fear of being discovered using drugs can therefore carry consequences extending well beyond parental disapproval, touching on family reputation, religious expectations and the policing of female behaviour.

Another revelation from our focus groups was the belief among some young people that nitrous oxide is somehow less haram — less religiously prohibited — than cannabis. Others regard it as less harmful to their health.

I am not interested in issuing religious verdicts, but I am interested in how these distinctions develop. Cannabis is widely recognised as a drug, whereas nitrous oxide, despite its intoxicating effects, can be dismissed as little more than gas in a balloon. It is not difficult to imagine how young people navigating a religious upbringing might construct their own hierarchy of what is acceptable.

The medical evidence offers no support for treating nitrous oxide as a harmless alternative to cannabis.

In fact, what researchers have discovered about its effects is deeply troubling.

A 2023 study published in the Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery & Psychiatry examined 119 patients suffering from nitrous oxide-related nerve and spinal cord injuries at hospitals in London, Birmingham and Greater Manchester. Their median age was just 22, and the youngest was 14.

What particularly caught my attention was the ethnic breakdown. Asian or Asian British patients accounted for 73% of cases in east London, 54% in Birmingham and 29% in Manchester. Across the study, they represented 57% of the patients.

These are striking figures, although they require careful interpretation. The research concerned patients who had already suffered neurological injuries, rather than a representative sample of nitrous oxide users. It does not establish that South Asian young people consume more of the drug than other groups, and the researchers could not determine why Asian patients appeared disproportionately represented.

Interestingly, the researchers themselves considered whether nitrous oxide might appeal to people who avoid other substances for cultural or religious reasons. Their findings did not conclusively support that explanation.

Nevertheless, the figures should concern anybody working with South Asian communities. They certainly concerned me.

Nitrous oxide produces a brief sensation of euphoria, dizziness or detachment, usually lasting only a few minutes. It is typically inhaled through balloons filled from pressurised cylinders. Yet repeated or heavy use can interfere with the body's ability to use vitamin B12, which is essential for maintaining healthy nerves and the spinal cord.

The consequences can be catastrophic. What begins as tingling fingers, numb feet or difficulty balancing can develop into severe weakness, impaired walking and, in some cases, permanent disability.

There is another misconception that deserves particular attention: the belief that taking vitamin B12 supplements can somehow protect people who continue inhaling nitrous oxide.

It is a dangerous assumption, and the evidence is unusually clear.

Nitrous oxide does not merely reduce the amount of vitamin B12 available in the body. It chemically inactivates the vitamin, interfering with the processes that keep the nervous system functioning properly. Simply swallowing B12 tablets while continuing to inhale the gas does not reliably prevent this damage.

In fact, the 2023 hospital study recorded that 13 of its 119 patients had been taking B12 supplements before presenting with neurological injuries.

The government's Advisory Council on the Misuse of Drugs has also reviewed evidence showing that neurological damage can develop despite supplementation. Its assessment concludes that B12 supplementation without stopping nitrous oxide appears ineffective at preventing these neurological disorders.

There is an important distinction between B12 as a medical treatment and B12 as a supposed licence to continue using the drug. Doctors can prescribe vitamin B12 injections as part of treatment for nitrous oxide-related neurological injury, alongside stopping use. That does not mean taking supplements makes recreational inhalation safe.

I wonder how many young people know this. And how many believe they have found a way of avoiding the consequences.

The risks extend beyond neurological injuries. In March 2026, 50-year-old Sylvester Abayomi was killed while travelling to work in Manchester when a Volkswagen Golf driven at more than 130mph crashed into his vehicle.

The driver, Uways Hussain, and his passenger, Usmon Mahmood, had filmed themselves inhaling nitrous oxide while the car was being driven at dangerous speeds. Both were subsequently jailed.

According to the Crown Prosecution Service, the men fled the scene after the collision, leaving Abayomi dying in his vehicle.

It is an appalling story, not least because it illustrates how quickly recreational drug use can have consequences for people who have made no decision to take part in it.

Meanwhile, the people supplying nitrous oxide unlawfully continue to provide another reason for concern.

In March 2026, Birmingham trading standards officers seized 68 large cylinders during an operation at a business in Sparkbrook. Two people were arrested. Despite the classification of nitrous oxide as a Class C drug in November 2023, unlawful supply remains a problem.

There are legitimate medical, catering and industrial uses for the gas. But those knowingly supplying it for recreational inhalation are operating outside the law.

I find it troubling that while public attention so often focuses on the young people inhaling balloons, considerably less attention is paid to those profiting from the trade. A cylinder changes hands, money is made, and the seller moves on. The consequences may remain with a family for the rest of their lives.

To be fair, the national picture is not one of relentlessly increasing use. Office for National Statistics figures for the year ending March 2025 suggest that nitrous oxide use among 16- to 24-year-olds fell from 3.3% to 1.3% over the previous year.

That decline is encouraging. But it does not diminish the severity of the injuries suffered by some users, or explain the disproportionate representation of young Asian patients in the hospital research.

Nor does it tell us much about the social world in which nitrous oxide is being consumed.

This is where I believe our experience in Rochdale has something important to contribute.

In our focus groups, we have found that young people's attitudes towards substances are often shaped by a complicated mixture of peer pressure, religious beliefs, family expectations and perceptions of risk. They are navigating a culture that is neither entirely that of their parents nor entirely separate from it.

A young person may regard alcohol as religiously forbidden, cannabis as socially unacceptable, and nitrous oxide as somehow occupying a different category. Another may understand the medical dangers but conceal their use because the fear of family discovery feels more immediate. Others may simply be looking for relief from difficult circumstances.

These differences matter.

Yet much of the mainstream substance use support system is organised around broad categories of need, with services commissioned to reach populations that may have very different experiences of addiction, family relationships and help-seeking.

I do not doubt the commitment of those working in these services. We have met dedicated professionals doing important work. But I do question whether sufficient attention is given to the cultural and religious circumstances in which young British Asians experience substance use.

It is not enough to translate a leaflet into Urdu or Punjabi and assume that a service has become culturally appropriate. Nor is it enough to hold an occasional awareness event in a mosque and consider the problem addressed.

We need practitioners who understand why a young woman might be frightened of her family discovering her drug use, why a teenager might rationalise nitrous oxide as less haram than cannabis, or why someone who has already been hospitalised repeatedly might still struggle to identify with the idea of recovery.

And we need to be willing to confront the role our own communities sometimes play in keeping addiction hidden.

The language of izzat and sharam — honour and shame — continues to shape how some families respond to substance use. The fear of bringing disgrace upon a household can discourage people from seeking help until their problems have become much more serious.

I have often wondered whether our obsession with protecting family reputations sometimes comes at the expense of protecting the people within them.

But I also think we must resist the temptation to explain everything through culture. The young man I met in Rochdale did not begin by talking about religion or honour. He talked about depression, his home life and the lack of things to do.

Those are not exclusively South Asian problems. They are questions about deprivation, opportunity and the emotional lives of young people growing up in towns where the promises of social mobility can feel increasingly remote.

His story reminds me that there are at least two conversations we need to have. One concerns the specific cultural circumstances in which some British South Asian young people encounter drugs and seek support. The other concerns the wider social conditions that make a few minutes of chemical escape so attractive in the first place.

Neither conversation is comfortable. Both are necessary.

When I think about that teenage girl in Rochdale, hurriedly discarding her balloon before disappearing indoors, I wonder what she understood about the risks, whether she felt able to speak openly with her family, and whether anybody would know how to respond if she needed help.

And when I think about the young man whose life has been permanently changed, I return to his explanation: I take it to feel better.

For all our concern about what young people are putting into their bodies, we seem remarkably reluctant to ask what is happening in their lives.

Perhaps that is where the conversation should begin.

The Salik Project UK is a Rochdale-based community organisation working to reduce stigma around substance use and improve access to culturally appropriate support. Identifying details of young people discussed in this article have been withheld to protect their privacy.
